Treatment for skin picking: what has evidence

Updated November 2026

Skin picking disorder can be treated. The evidence is still smaller than for depression or OCD, but it points the same way.

Habit reversal training

The NHS and the MSD Manual both name cognitive behavioral therapy built around habit reversal training as the treatment of choice. It has three parts:

  • Awareness: noticing when you pick, and what comes right before (a place, a feeling, a sensation).
  • Stimulus control: changing the setting so picking is harder to start, like covering mirrors, dimming the light, trimming nails, keeping tweezers away.
  • Competing response: doing something with your hands that can't go with picking, like clenching a fist, holding something, sitting on your hands, until the urge passes.

In a 2006 randomised trial, adults who had habit reversal picked significantly less than a waiting-list group, after treatment and three months later. A 2019 systematic review found the trials so far are few and small, so more research is needed. It's still the best-supported option.

CBT more broadly

Therapists often add work on the thoughts and feelings around picking: shame, perfectionism, "I've ruined it now so I might as well keep going". More in CBT for skin picking.

Medication

Some medicines have been studied for skin picking, with mixed and limited results. Whether any is right for you is a question for a doctor or psychiatrist, not a website.

Skin care

If wounds are open, infected or not healing, see a GP or dermatologist. The NHS also suggests caring for the skin when the urge comes, for example putting on moisturiser instead of picking.
