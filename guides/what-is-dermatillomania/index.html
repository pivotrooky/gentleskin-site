What is dermatillomania?

Updated October 2026 · 5 min read

Dermatillomania is repeated picking at your own skin that you find hard to stop, even when it leaves marks and you've tried to quit. Its clinical name is excoriation disorder, or skin picking disorder. It's one of the body-focused repetitive behaviors (BFRBs), the same family as hair pulling (trichotillomania) and nail biting.

Almost everyone picks at a scab or a rough cuticle now and then. It becomes dermatillomania when it's frequent, hard to control, and starts to cost you something: skin damage, time, or the feeling of hiding it.

What it looks like

  • Picking, scratching, squeezing or digging at skin: the face, scalp, lips, cuticles, arms, legs, back or chest.
  • Going after bumps, scabs, spots or rough patches, or "perfectly fine" skin that feels uneven.
  • Picking without noticing (while reading, scrolling, driving), or on purpose in front of a mirror, sometimes for a long time.
  • Trying to cut down or stop, and finding it comes back.
  • Covering marks with makeup or clothes, or avoiding situations because of them.

Psychiatrists list excoriation disorder with the obsessive-compulsive and related disorders. In plain terms, it's diagnosed when picking causes skin lesions, you've repeatedly tried to stop, and it causes real distress or gets in the way of life, and it isn't better explained by a skin condition, a drug or another disorder.

How common is it?

More common than most people think. Studies estimate that somewhere between 1 and 5 adults in 100 have it, and many never tell anyone. In clinical samples most people with it are women, and it often starts in the teens.

Why do people pick?

There's no single cause, and it isn't a lack of willpower. People describe picking as:

  • Soothing: it takes the edge off stress, anxiety or boredom.
  • Automatic: the hands go there on their own during quiet, focused moments.
  • Satisfying: getting something out, or making a surface feel smooth, gives a moment of relief.

That relief is what keeps it going: the picking works, briefly, so the brain learns to reach for it. Afterwards often come regret and shame, which add to the stress that sets off the next round.

Is it dangerous?

Picking can lead to open wounds, infections and scarring, so see a doctor about any wound that looks infected or won't heal. The emotional side matters too: hiding it, avoiding people, feeling ashamed. Both are good reasons to look for support, not reasons for more shame.

Can it get better?

Yes. The best-studied approach is a kind of cognitive behavioral therapy built around habit reversal: noticing when and why you pick, then giving your hands and your feelings something else to do.
