Skin picking and ADHD

Updated November 2026

In shortStudies find ADHD more often in people with skin picking disorder than in people without it, but that's an association: they don't show that ADHD causes picking, and plenty of people who pick don't have ADHD. Habit reversal is still the main treatment for picking. If you have both, tell whoever treats each one about the other.

If you have ADHD and pick your skin, you're not imagining a link. Researchers keep finding the two together. What they haven't worked out is why.

What studies have found

  • In 262 adults with skin picking disorder assessed in person for research studies, other conditions were common, and ADHD and OCD were the most frequent of the impulsive and compulsive ones.
  • A US study compared the medical records of 250 patients with skin picking disorder at one hospital with 250 matched patients without it. ADHD was recorded more often in the picking group, as were depression, anxiety, OCD and several other conditions. The estimate was imprecise: the odds of ADHD were about 5.6 times higher, with a range from about 1.6 to 20.
  • In a survey of people with hair pulling disorder, skin picking disorder or both, between 12% and 32% of each group said they had ADHD.

The TLC Foundation for BFRBs sums it up as two conditions that often occur together, with a relationship that isn't fully understood.

What they haven't shown

These studies show that the two turn up together more often than chance. They don't show that ADHD causes skin picking, or the other way round. The people in them came from clinics, research studies or online surveys, who may not be typical of everyone who picks.

There are ideas about why they overlap: impulsivity, trouble regulating emotions, and differences in how the body's sensations are processed. A team in Basel, Switzerland proposed this kind of shared explanation after treating three women with long-term itch, ADHD and skin picking, and they present it as a hypothesis, not a finding.

What it means for you

  • The treatment for picking is the same. Habit reversal training and CBT are the approaches with the most evidence for skin picking, with or without ADHD. For people with both, the TLC Foundation suggests combining therapy and medication when needed. See treatments that have evidence.
  • Use things that work without remembering. Plasters or gloves on the fingers you pick with, short nails, and a fidget left in the places you pick don't depend on catching yourself in the moment. Focused and automatic picking has more.
  • Ask about medication, don't guess. There are single case reports going both ways: one patient whose skin picking was successfully treated with methylphenidate for ADHD, and one child whose picking started on atomoxetine. One case can't tell you what will happen to you. Tell the doctor who prescribes your ADHD medicine about the picking.
  • Tell both sides. A therapist working on picking should know about ADHD, and the doctor treating ADHD should know about the picking.
