Cuticle and finger picking

Updated November 2026

In shortCuticle picking is picking, peeling or biting the cuticles, hangnails and skin around your nails, often without noticing. It's a body-focused repetitive behavior, close to nail biting, and torn skin there can get infected (paronychia). Leaving the cuticles in place, moisturising your hands, short nails, covering the fingers you pick, and something else for your hands all help.

Cuticle picking means picking, peeling or biting the cuticles and the skin at the sides of your nails. A hangnail or a rough edge usually starts it, and your other fingers find it easily, often while your attention is somewhere else. The TLC Foundation for BFRBs files biting the cuticles or fingers under skin biting, one of the body-focused repetitive behaviors, and notes that it often goes together with nail biting.

Why it keeps going

Your fingertips are always within reach of each other, at a desk, on the phone, in a meeting, in bed. Every torn edge leaves a new rough edge, and the skin around it dries and cracks, so there's nearly always something to find. Like other picking, getting a loose bit off feels satisfying for a moment, and that is what keeps the habit going.

What it can do

Torn skin around a nail lets germs in. MedlinePlus lists biting off or picking a hangnail, and trimming or pushing back the cuticle, as causes of paronychia, an infection of the skin around the nail that shows up as a painful, red, swollen area. The TLC Foundation lists damage to the cuticles and nails among the complications of BFRBs. The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) notes that repeated nail biting can make the skin around the nails sore and damage the tissue that makes nails grow.

What helps

  • Leave the cuticles in place. The AAD advises not to remove them, and to moisturise after washing your hands. Less dry, cracked skin means fewer edges to catch.
  • Keep nails short and smooth. Less nail gives you less to pick with.
  • Cover the fingers you use. Plasters, tape or thin gloves at the times it's worst. Both the NHS and the AAD suggest gloves or covers.
  • Know your triggers. The AAD names physical ones, like hangnails, and feelings, like boredom, stress or anxiety. Note when and where you pick for a week or two.
  • Give your hands a job. A stress ball or a fidget where you usually pick, used every time you catch your fingers at your nails.
  • Go one finger at a time. The AAD suggests stopping nail biting gradually, starting with one set of nails, and the same idea works for picking.
  • Wait out the urge. Urge surfing helps you let it pass. How to stop picking your skin covers the whole method.

When to see someone

See a doctor if the skin around a nail is red, swollen and painful and isn't getting better, or is getting worse. If picking is taking up a lot of your time or you can't stop, a therapist who treats BFRBs can help. See how to find help.
