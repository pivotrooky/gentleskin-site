Picking at pimples

Updated November 2026

In shortSqueezing a pimple now and then doesn't make it a disorder. It becomes skin picking disorder when you pick again and again, it leaves sores or scars, you've tried to stop and can't, and it causes you distress; dermatologists call picked acne acne excoriée. Picking pushes the contents deeper and can bring more inflammation, infection and scars, so it helps to treat both the acne and the picking.

Lots of people with acne squeeze a spot now and then. For some people it goes further: hours at the mirror, picking at spots that aren't ready or at skin with no spots at all, and marks that last longer than the acne ever did. The NHS lists skin conditions like acne among the things that set off skin picking, and for some people the acne and the picking keep each other going.

When it's more than popping a pimple

Skin picking disorder (dermatillomania) is diagnosed when all of these are true: the picking causes skin lesions, you've tried to cut down or stop more than once, it causes distress or gets in the way of your life, and something else, like a medical condition, doesn't explain it better. Having acne doesn't rule it out.

Dermatologists call scratched or picked pimples acne excoriée. DermNet, a dermatology reference, describes it as more common in women, particularly with acne that starts later in life, and says some people have few or no active spots, only the sores, dark marks and scars left by picking. A 2024 review describes it as a form of skin picking disorder that typically appears in adolescence or young adulthood, and says it's under-recognised because people don't mention the picking, or don't notice it.

Some things that point past the odd squeeze:

  • You pick at spots that aren't ready, or search for ones that aren't there.
  • You lose a lot of time at the mirror, sometimes with tweezers or pins.
  • The marks from picking bother you more than the acne.
  • You keep picking after the acne has cleared up.

Why picking makes it worse

The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) explains that squeezing often pushes some of what's inside a pimple deeper into the skin, which increases inflammation. It lists permanent acne scars, more noticeable acne, more painful acne and infection from the bacteria on your hands as possible results. The NHS says not to pick or squeeze spots because it can cause scars. A spot that looks worse after picking is one more thing to pick, which is how the habit feeds itself.

What helps

  • Treat the acne. Fewer spots means fewer triggers. The AAD says many people can treat acne with over-the-counter products and that a dermatologist can help if you don't see results in 4 to 6 weeks. The NHS suggests seeing a GP if spots are deep, painful or scarring, or if acne is affecting your mental health.
  • Tell the doctor about the picking. DermNet notes that treatment depends on it: some people mainly need to stop the habit, and some need psychological therapy as well as acne treatment.
  • Change the mirror. Dim the light, put the magnifying mirror away, keep tweezers and pins out of reach, and set a timer before you go in. More in how to stop picking your skin.
  • Do something else for a sore spot. For a painful pimple, the AAD suggests ice to bring down the inflammation, instead of squeezing.
  • Treat the picking too. In a small randomised trial of 32 adults with acne excoriée, those who had eight sessions of CBT alongside their acne medication improved more, in picking and in their skin, than those who had medication alone. CBT for skin picking explains what that involves.
