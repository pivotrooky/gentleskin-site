Lip picking

Updated November 2026

In shortLip picking is pulling or biting off dry skin on your lips, often without noticing, until they're sore or bleed. It belongs to the same family as skin picking, the body-focused repetitive behaviors (BFRBs), and it feeds itself: picking slows healing and leaves new rough edges to find. Keeping your lips smooth, noticing when it happens, and giving your hands something else to do all help.

Lip picking means pulling at dry or flaky skin on your lips with your fingers or your teeth. It often starts with one rough edge that feels like it has to go, and it can end with lips that are sore, bleeding or swollen. The TLC Foundation for BFRBs counts lip biting among the body-focused repetitive behaviors, the family that skin picking and hair pulling belong to.

Why the lips

Your lips are always within reach of your fingers and teeth, and you can feel a rough patch without a mirror. Dry or chapped lips give you edges to pull. Pulling skin off before it's ready leaves a raw spot, which dries and flakes again, so there's soon something new to find. The NHS advises against picking or biting flaky skin on your lips because it slows down healing.

Like other picking, it often happens while you read, scroll, drive or think, and you only notice when it stings. Some people do it on purpose at a mirror, going after every loose bit. Most do some of both. Focused and automatic picking explains why the difference matters.

It rarely comes alone

In an online survey of 391 people with BFRBs, about a third reported lip or cheek biting, and nearly three in four had more than one BFRB. If you pick your lips, you may also bite your nails or the inside of your cheeks, or pick your skin somewhere else.

What helps

  • Keep your lips smooth. The NHS suggests a lip balm with petroleum jelly or beeswax, put on with clean hands, and not licking dry lips, which makes them sore. Smooth lips give your fingers less to find.
  • Reach for the balm instead. When the urge comes, putting balm on the spot gives your hand a job that looks after the skin. The NHS suggests moisturising as one of the things to do instead of picking.
  • Find out when it happens. For a week or two, note each time: what you were doing and where. Lip picking tends to come back in the same few situations, like a screen, a book, the car or a stressful call.
  • Keep your hands busy there. A fidget, a pen or a mug in the places you pick. If it's your teeth, sugar-free gum keeps your mouth busy.
  • Wait out the urge. It rises and falls on its own. Urge surfing is a way to watch it pass.

When to see someone

The NHS says to see a GP if your lips are hot, painful and swollen, which can be a sign of infection. Lips that stay crusted or peeling for weeks are worth showing a doctor too: picking, biting and licking can keep a long-lasting peeling of the lips (exfoliative cheilitis) going. If picking takes up a lot of your time or you can't stop, a therapist who treats BFRBs can help. See how to find help.
