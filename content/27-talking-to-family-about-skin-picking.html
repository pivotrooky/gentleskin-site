Talking to family and friends about skin picking

Updated November 2026

Most people with dermatillomania hide it, sometimes for years. Telling someone can take a lot of weight off, and the NHS lists it among the things that help: other people can help you notice when you're picking.

What to tell them

  • It has a name: skin picking disorder, or dermatillomania. It's in the manual psychiatrists use.
  • It's common: about 2 to 3 in 100 people.
  • It isn't vanity and it isn't self-harm. It's a habit that relieves something for a moment and is very hard to stop. (More on that.)
  • It isn't anyone's fault, including theirs if they're your parent.

What to ask for

Be specific. People want to help and often guess wrong. Some things that tend to help:

  • A quiet, agreed signal when they see you picking, if you want one, instead of "stop that!"
  • Not commenting on your skin.
  • Company or a distraction at the times it's worst.
  • Patience: slips are part of getting better.

And what doesn't: slapping hands away, nagging, checking your skin, or making it about willpower.

If you're the parent

Getting frustrated or frightened when the picking continues is normal. The TLC Foundation says BFRBs come from a mix of biology, genes and environment, not from "bad parenting", and it has guidance on getting treatment for a child. Treat it as something you face together, not something to police.
