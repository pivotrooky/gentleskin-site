CBT for skin picking

Updated October 2026

Cognitive behavioral therapy, or CBT, is the talking therapy with the most research behind it for skin picking. For picking it isn't mostly talk. The core is habit reversal training, a set of practical steps for noticing picking and doing something else, plus work on the thoughts and feelings that keep it going. Many therapists also bring in ideas from acceptance-based therapies.

Habit reversal

Habit reversal training has three main parts.

Awareness. You learn exactly when, where and how you pick: the spots, the times, what your hands do first, what you were feeling. Usually that means keeping a record between sessions.

A competing response. You pick something to do with your hands that makes picking impossible, like making loose fists or holding a fidget, and do it for a minute or so whenever you notice an urge or catch yourself picking.

Support. Someone you trust notices when you're picking and reminds you gently, and notices when you're doing well.

The ComB model

Many BFRB specialists use the Comprehensive Behavioral model, or ComB. It looks at five areas that can drive picking: what you feel physically (a bump, an itch), what you think, your emotions, what your hands are doing, and the place you're in. You and the therapist work out which matter most for you and choose strategies for each. Someone who picks at the mirror after a stressful day needs a different plan from someone who picks without noticing while reading.

Thoughts that give you permission

A lot of picking starts with a thought that makes it seem fine just this once. Some common ones:

  • "Just this one, then I'll stop."
  • "It'll look better once I get it out."
  • "I've already started, so I might as well finish."
  • "I've had an awful day, I deserve it."

In CBT you learn to catch these thoughts, notice how they've played out before, and answer them. For example: "Last time it was never just one, and the spot looked worse." The aim isn't to argue yourself into a perfect mood. It's to notice the moment the thought shows up, so you have a choice.

CBT also works on thoughts about your skin itself: that a spot is all anyone sees, or that your skin has to be perfectly smooth. Those thoughts can keep you at the mirror and make you hide from people.

Acceptance and emotions

Trying to force an urge away often makes it stronger. Acceptance-based approaches teach you to notice an urge or a hard feeling and let it be there without acting on it. Urge surfing is one example. When picking is mainly a way of coping with stress or difficult emotions, therapy also helps you find other ways to calm down.

What sessions are like

Sessions are practical. You go over your records, try strategies, and leave with something to practise before the next one. Many therapists work online, which helps because BFRB specialists are hard to find in some places. Slips are normal and expected, and a good therapist will treat them as information, not failure.

Finding a therapist

Look for someone who has treated body-focused repetitive behaviors before and uses habit reversal or the ComB model. A general CBT therapist can help too, especially if they're willing to learn the BFRB approach. The TLC Foundation for BFRBs has a directory at bfrb.org.
