How to stop picking your skin

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Short version: you don't stop skin picking by trying harder. You stop it by noticing when it happens, making it harder to start, and giving your hands and feelings something else to do when the urge comes. That's the core of habit reversal training, the approach therapists use most for skin picking and other body-focused repetitive behaviors.

Here it is in steps you can start today.

1. Notice when and where it happens

A lot of picking is automatic: your fingers find a bump while you're reading or on your phone, and you only notice afterwards. The first step is simply catching it. For a week or two, note each time you pick:

  • Where on your body: face, scalp, lips, cuticles.
  • When: time of day, and what you were doing.
  • How aware you were: did you decide to, or did you find yourself already doing it?
  • How you felt just before: stressed, bored, tired, focused.

Patterns show up fast. Many people find most of their picking happens in two or three situations: the bathroom mirror at night, in bed, at the desk. Those are where to start. (A tracker makes this easier; see why track your skin picking.)

2. Make picking harder to start

Once you know your situations, change them so picking takes more effort:

  • The mirror: dim the bathroom light, cover or skip the magnifying mirror, set a timer for how long you stay.
  • Your hands: keep nails short and smooth, wear gloves or finger cots in high-risk moments, use bandages over spots you go back to.
  • Your skin: moisturize rough patches and treat the skin issues that draw you in, like acne or dry scalp, with a doctor's help.
  • Your setting: keep tweezers out of reach, sit away from mirrors, keep something in your hands while you watch or read.

3. Give your hands something else to do

This is the "competing response" in habit reversal: an action you can do instead of picking, that uses the same hands and can't happen at the same time. Close your hands into gentle fists for a minute, press your palms together, squeeze a stress ball, roll a fidget toy, run your fingers over something with texture. Pick one or two and use them every time you notice the urge or catch yourself mid-pick.

4. Ride out the urge instead of fighting it

Urges feel like they'll keep growing until you give in, but they don't. They rise, peak and fade, usually within minutes. Instead of gritting your teeth, watch the urge like a wave: notice where you feel it, breathe, and let it crest and pass. This is called urge surfing, and it gets easier each time you do it.

5. Look after what's underneath

Picking often does a job: it calms stress, fills boredom, or takes the edge off a hard feeling. If you take it away without replacing that job, it tends to come back. Think about what else does that job for you: a walk, a shower, moving your body, a few slow breaths, talking to someone, going to bed earlier.

6. Expect slips, and count your wins

Almost everyone who cuts down on picking has days where it comes back. A slip isn't a reset to zero, and shaming yourself for it usually feeds the next round. What matters is the trend over weeks. Count the urges you got through, not only the picks.

When to get help

If picking is causing wounds, infections or scars, or taking a lot of your time and peace of mind, a therapist who knows body-focused repetitive behaviors can help a lot. Look for CBT with habit reversal training or the Comprehensive Behavioral (ComB) model; the TLC Foundation for BFRBs lists therapists at bfrb.org. See a doctor about any wound that looks infected.
